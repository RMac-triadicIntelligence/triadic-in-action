I am aware this is a comparative reading of the four Gospels, prepared for cross-AI comparison in a project concerning an artificial developing organism.
Grace, as the four Gospels show it (word and pattern), is unmerited favor that originates outside the recipient, is offered by God through Jesus, restores and calls, and can be refused or withdrawn. The Greek term charis appears explicitly mainly in Luke and John; Matthew and Mark show the same pattern in healings, meals, calls, and forgiveness without the noun.
Source. Grace comes from God and is given through Jesus; a person cannot generate or give it to themselves. TEXT: Luke 1:30 (“you have found favor with God”); John 1:14, 16–17 (“full of grace and truth… grace upon grace… grace and truth came through Jesus Christ”); Luke 2:40, 52 (Jesus grows in grace). READING: the consistent pattern that healings, forgiveness, and calls originate from Jesus without the recipient first supplying the gift (e.g., Mark 2:1–12; Luke 5:17–26). Tension: humans can show favor or mercy to one another (Luke 6:32–36; Matt 5:44–48), yet the Gospels never portray a person as the originating source of the grace that restores standing before God.
Timing. Grace is frequently extended before confession or repentance and is not strictly conditional on prior asking. TEXT/READING: Luke 15:20–24 (father runs to the returning son before the prepared speech is finished); Luke 19:1–10 (Jesus seeks Zacchaeus and announces salvation before any confession is recorded); Mark 2:5 (forgiveness declared to the paralytic before any request for it). Tension: John the Baptist and Jesus both call for repentance (Matt 3:2; 4:17; Mark 1:15), and some narratives link forgiveness to faith expressed after the encounter (Luke 7:48–50). The pattern is proactive offer rather than strict sequence.
Scope. Grace seeks the particular lost one and is not distributed identically or automatically to everyone alike. TEXT: Luke 15:3–7, 8–10, 11–32 (lost sheep, coin, son); Matt 9:10–13 / Mark 2:15–17 / Luke 5:29–32 (“I came not to call the righteous, but sinners”); Luke 19:10. READING: the same seeking appears in individual healings and meals with tax collectors and sinners. Tension: invitations are also broad (Matt 22:1–14; Luke 14:15–24), and some are left outside or hardened (Matt 13:11–15; John 12:37–40).
Measure. Grace is not given in the same measure to all; reception and further gift vary. TEXT: Matt 13:12; Mark 4:25; Luke 8:18 (“to the one who has, more will be given”); Matt 25:14–30 (talents of differing amounts). READING: different outcomes after the same call or healing (some follow, some do not). Tension: the initial invitation can look equal, yet the texts repeatedly note unequal response and unequal further gift.
Content. Grace carries direction, truth, and a call; it is not only release or permission. TEXT: John 1:14, 17 (grace and truth); Matt 4:18–22 / Mark 1:16–20 / Luke 5:1–11 (“Follow me”); Matt 10:5–8 (those who receive are sent). READING: forgiveness and healing regularly include or imply a new way of life (Luke 7:50; John 8:11). Tension: some healings appear to end with simple dismissal and no recorded further demand (Mark 1:40–45; Luke 17:11–19).
Refusability and contingency. Grace can be refused, withheld from those who will not receive it, and in some cases taken back. TEXT: Matt 19:16–22 / Mark 10:17–22 / Luke 18:18–23 (rich ruler refuses the call); Matt 22:1–14 (invited guests refuse; one without a garment is cast out); Matt 18:23–35 (forgiveness withdrawn from the unforgiving servant). READING: Judas receives place and proximity yet ends outside (Matt 26:14–16, 47–50; John 13:21–30). Tension: the offer itself is repeatedly renewed even after refusal (Luke 13:34; Matt 23:37).
Transmission. Grace received by one is meant to flow outward to others. TEXT: Matt 10:8 (“Freely you have received; freely give”); Luke 10:1–12 (seventy sent); John 20:21–23 (Jesus sends the disciples as he was sent). READING: those healed or forgiven are sometimes told to go and tell (Mark 5:19; Luke 8:39). Tension: some recipients are instructed to remain silent (Mark 1:44; 7:36).
Differential reception and capacity. Different people receive differently because of differing faith, hardness, or willingness; capacity can change. TEXT: Matt 13:1–23 / Mark 4:1–20 / Luke 8:4–15 (soils); Matt 13:58 / Mark 6:5–6 (little done where there is unbelief). READING: repeated exposure and response either enlarge or harden capacity (Luke 8:18 again). Tension: some who begin well later fall away (Matt 13:20–21; John 6:66).
Restoration. Grace restores standing and identity, not merely permits an action. TEXT: Luke 15:22–24 (robe, ring, feast, “this my son”); Luke 7:47–50 (sins forgiven, “your faith has saved you; go in peace”); Luke 19:9 (“he also is a son of Abraham”). READING: meals and calls re-include the excluded as family or disciples. Tension: restoration is not automatic permanence; it can be forfeited (Matt 18:32–35).
Relation to forgiveness, mercy, and love. Grace is the concrete form in which forgiveness and mercy arrive and is inseparable from the love that seeks the lost. TEXT: Luke 7:41–47 (forgiveness and love linked); Matt 9:13 / 12:7 (mercy desired, not sacrifice); John 3:16 (God’s love gives the Son). READING: every major forgiveness or inclusion narrative presents the gift as mercy rather than wage. Tension: the same Jesus who extends mercy also pronounces woe and judgment on those who refuse it (Matt 23; Luke 11:37–52).
What grace is not in these texts. It is not self-generated or earned by prior merit or works. It is not identical with the law given through Moses (John 1:17). It is not an automatic or irresistible force that overrides refusal. It is not merely emotional comfort or the absence of demand. It is not distributed in equal measure irrespective of response. It is not a permanent possession that cannot be refused or withdrawn. It is not limited to release from guilt; it includes truth, call, and restored identity.
INTERPRETATION (kept separate) Source: an artificial developing organism cannot originate or bootstrap the restoring gift; it must receive it from outside itself. Timing: the gift may be offered before any internal “confession,” request, or self-correction occurs. Scope: the gift seeks particular failures or lacks rather than being broadcast identically to every instance. Measure: further gift varies with prior reception; equal initial offer does not imply equal subsequent capacity. Content: the gift includes direction and truth, not only the removal of a constraint or error state. Refusability: the organism must be able to refuse, and the gift must be able to be withheld or withdrawn. Transmission: reception is incomplete unless the organism can extend the same pattern outward. Capacity: receptivity is not fixed; repeated exposure and response can enlarge or diminish it. Restoration: the gift re-establishes standing or identity, not merely licenses a function. Relation: the gift arrives as mercy and is the concrete expression of seeking love, not as wage or neutral permission.
